Asia’s First National Park (Est. 1936)

A Conservation Legacy Spanning Nearly a Century

Established on 8 August 1936 as Hailey National Park, the protected area is recognised as India’s first national park and the first national park on mainland Asia. It was renamed Ramganga National Park in the mid-1950s and subsequently became Corbett National Park in memory of Edward James (Jim) Corbett, the naturalist, author and conservation advocate associated with the area. Historical sources differ slightly on the exact year of the intermediate name changes; the present name was in use by 1957.

Jim Corbett was a legendary British-Indian naturalist, author of "Man-Eaters of Kumaon", and a pioneer conservationist who dedicated his later life to championing photographic safaris over hunting trophies. In 1973, this park served as the prestigious launchpad for India’s historic Project Tiger.

Location, Terrain & Natural Setting

Geography of Jim Corbett National Park

Corbett lies where the Himalayan foothills descend into the Bhabar and Terai plains. This transition creates steep hills, forested valleys, river corridors and open grasslands within one connected landscape.

Where is Corbett Tiger Reserve located?

Corbett is in the Kumaon region of Uttarakhand, northern India, with its main visitor and administrative gateway at Ramnagar in Nainital district. The wider tiger-reserve landscape extends across parts of Nainital, Pauri Garhwal and the adjoining Almora-side forest landscape. Ramnagar is the principal road and rail access point for many visitors, while individual safari gates are located at different distances from town.

The protected landscape is not a single uniform block. It includes the national park, Sonanadi Wildlife Sanctuary and surrounding reserve-forest areas. The National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) lists the notified Corbett Tiger Reserve at 1,288.31 sq. km, comprising 821.99 sq. km of core/critical tiger habitat and 466.32 sq. km of buffer/peripheral area. The national-park core and the wider tiger reserve are different administrative/geographical units, so area figures should not be used interchangeably.

Relief, elevation and landforms

The terrain rises from the plains towards the outer Himalayan foothills. It includes the lower Shivalik hills, ridges, ravines, narrow valleys, seasonal stream channels, broad valley floors and flatter alluvial stretches. Elevation varies substantially across the reserve; the NTCA describes a foothill landscape with undulating terrain and several river valleys. This variation in slope, soil moisture, aspect and elevation helps explain why forest composition and wildlife habitats change over relatively short distances.

The southern Bhabar belt is characterised by coarse sediments, pebbles and boulders through which streams can disappear underground and re-emerge farther downslope. Towards the Terai, finer alluvial deposits and a shallower water table support wetter soils, marshy patches and productive grasslands. These adjoining ecological zones are important to the movement of water, plants and animals.

Rivers, streams and the Ramganga reservoir

The Ramganga is the principal river system associated with the park’s central landscape. It flows through forested valleys and the Dhikala landscape before entering the large reservoir formed by the Kalagarh dam. The reservoir, created in the 1970s, changed parts of the valley by submerging some former riverine and grassland habitat while creating an extensive waterbody used by aquatic birds and other wildlife.

Other important rivers and streams in the wider reserve include the Sonanadi, Palain/Pallaen and Mandal, along with numerous seasonal nalas and smaller tributaries. The Kosi River flows along the eastern edge of the Corbett landscape near Ramnagar and nearby settlements. River levels and stream flow vary with rainfall and season; monsoon runoff can make some routes and crossings unsafe or inaccessible.

Climate and seasons

Corbett has a strongly seasonal climate shaped by its foothill location. Winters are generally cool, especially during early-morning safaris. The pre-monsoon and summer months are hotter, with temperatures rising considerably in exposed areas. The southwest monsoon brings most of the annual rainfall, replenishes streams and grasslands, and can cause road or trail closures because of swollen rivers, erosion and landslides in the wider region.

These seasonal changes affect visibility, vegetation, water availability and animal movement. Dry-season water sources may concentrate wildlife activity around rivers and pools, but no season or safari zone guarantees a tiger sighting. Tourism opening dates are notified by the Forest Department and can change; always verify current gate status before travel.

Forests, Grasslands & Riverine Habitat

Forests and Natural Habitats of Corbett

Corbett’s biodiversity is supported by a patchwork of sal forest, mixed deciduous woodland, riverine vegetation, scrub, grassland and aquatic habitat.

Sal and mixed deciduous forests

Sal (Shorea robusta) is a characteristic tree across large parts of the reserve. It occurs alongside mixed deciduous species, including trees such as haldu, rohini, jamun, amla and various figs. Forest composition changes with altitude, slope, soil and moisture.

Riverine forests and wetlands

Riverbanks, pools, sandbars and damp depressions support grasses, reeds, shrubs and moisture-loving trees. These areas provide feeding and resting habitat for birds, reptiles, fish and mammals, and act as important movement corridors.

Chaurs and grasslands

Open grasslands known locally as chaurs occur in valley bottoms and selected clearings. Dhikala Chaur is among the best-known examples. Grasslands support grazing herbivores and create open viewing areas, but their ecology depends on water, fire, grazing and habitat management.

Shivalik slopes and scrub

Hilly slopes, ridges and drier south-facing terrain support different woodland and scrub communities. Seasonal streams cut through these slopes, creating sheltered microhabitats and varied conditions for wildlife.

Why habitat diversity matters

A tiger reserve needs more than a forest canopy. Tigers depend on prey populations, cover, water and connected territories. Elephants use broad movement routes and a range of feeding habitats. Deer and other herbivores depend on grass, browse and water. Birds use forest layers, river edges, cliffs, wetlands and open ground. Maintaining this habitat mosaic—and the connections between it—is central to conservation in Corbett.

Wildlife of the Terai–Shivalik Landscape

Animals, Birds and Aquatic Life in Corbett

Corbett is known for its large mammals, but its ecological importance also comes from its birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects and plant life.

Large mammals and the food web

The Royal Bengal tiger is the best-known predator of Corbett, but it shares the landscape with leopards, jungle cats and other smaller carnivores. Asian elephants move through forest and grassland habitats, often in family groups. Important herbivores include chital (spotted deer), sambar, barking deer, hog deer and wild boar. These prey species form a major part of the food web and influence vegetation through grazing and browsing.

Other mammals recorded in the wider landscape include sloth bears, otters, jackals, foxes, civets, mongooses, langurs and macaques. Species distribution differs across the reserve; not every animal is present in every tourism zone or visible on every safari.

Birdlife

Corbett is an important birding destination because it combines Himalayan foothill forest, riverine habitat, grassland and reservoir edges. Birdwatchers may encounter hornbills, eagles, vultures, owls, kingfishers, woodpeckers, barbets, parakeets, pheasants, waterbirds and seasonal migrants. The exact number of recorded species varies by checklist and survey date; official and conservation references commonly report well over 500 species.

Reptiles, amphibians and freshwater life

The river and wetland habitats support reptiles such as gharials in suitable river stretches, mugger crocodiles, turtles, monitor lizards and snakes. The Ramganga system also supports a range of freshwater fish, including the celebrated golden mahseer in appropriate stretches. Amphibians and aquatic invertebrates are part of the food web and are sensitive indicators of water and habitat quality.

Conservation is bigger than a single species

Protecting the tiger also means protecting prey, rivers, grasslands, forest corridors and the wider landscape shared with local communities. Responsible tourism should keep disturbance low, follow route and timing rules, avoid litter and never pressure guides or drivers to approach or chase animals.

The Person Behind the Name

Who Was Jim Corbett?

The park is named after Edward James Corbett, widely known as Jim Corbett, whose life and legacy are closely associated with the forests of Kumaon.

Edward James Corbett (1875–1955) was born in Nainital and spent much of his life in the Kumaon region. He became known for tracking and killing man-eating tigers and leopards that posed a serious threat to people in particular historical circumstances. He later wrote accounts of these experiences, including Man-Eaters of Kumaon, which brought the forests and villages of the region to a wide readership.

Corbett’s legacy is more complex than that of a hunter alone. He also photographed and observed wildlife, argued for the protection of forests and animals, and supported the idea that wild habitats should be conserved. His name was given to the national park in recognition of his association with the region and his role in the early conservation story.

The modern reserve is managed under contemporary wildlife laws and conservation science. It should not be understood as a memorial to hunting: its purpose today is habitat protection, biodiversity conservation, research, regulated tourism and the long-term survival of wildlife.

A Timeline of Protection

History of Corbett National Park and Tiger Reserve

From early forest protection to the launch of Project Tiger, Corbett has played a major role in the development of wildlife conservation in India.

Before 1936

  • The forests and valleys were part of a lived-in Kumaon landscape, used by local communities and shaped by colonial-era forest administration.
  • Early proposals for a protected park emerged in the early twentieth century.

8 August 1936

  • Hailey National Park was established, named after Sir Malcolm Hailey.
  • It became the first national park in India and mainland Asia.

1950s

  • The park was renamed Ramganga National Park in the mid-1950s.
  • It was subsequently renamed Corbett National Park in honour of Jim Corbett; official histories commonly record the present name by 1957.

1 April 1973

  • Project Tiger was launched at Corbett as one of the original tiger reserves in the national programme.
  • The initiative focused on protecting tigers and the habitats and prey base on which they depend.

1991 onwards

  • Additional surrounding forest and sanctuary areas were brought into the wider reserve landscape.
  • Sonanadi Wildlife Sanctuary and adjoining reserve forests form part of the broader conservation unit.

26 February 2010

  • Corbett Tiger Reserve was formally notified by the Government of Uttarakhand under the Wildlife (Protection) Act framework.
  • The present reserve is managed as a core and buffer landscape covering 1,288.31 sq. km in the NTCA profile.

Protecting a Connected Landscape

Conservation Challenges and Responsible Tourism

Long-term conservation depends on healthy habitats, connected wildlife corridors, careful visitor management and cooperation with communities around the reserve.

Habitat connectivity and human–wildlife coexistence

Corbett forms part of the wider Terai Arc landscape. Wildlife does not recognise administrative boundaries, and elephants, tigers and other species need connected habitat beyond the tourism zones. Roads, settlements, agriculture and other land uses can affect movement routes and increase the chance of human–wildlife encounters.

Fire, invasive plants and changing river systems

Forest fires are a natural ecological process in some habitats, but severe or frequent fires can damage regeneration and wildlife cover. Invasive plants can alter grassland and woodland composition. River flow, sediment movement and reservoir management also influence aquatic and riverine habitats. These issues require ongoing monitoring and adaptive management.

How visitors can help

  • Use only authorised gates, routes, vehicles and registered guides.
  • Never feed, call, chase or attempt to approach wild animals.
  • Remain inside the vehicle unless the authorised staff direct otherwise at a permitted stop.
  • Keep noise low, do not use flash where prohibited and take all litter back out.
  • Respect local communities, forest staff and other visitors.
  • Do not treat sightings as guaranteed; the forest is a living ecosystem, not a performance.

Flora, Fauna & Ecosystems

Rich Biodiversity: What Wildlife Lives in Corbett?

Corbett is a varied Himalayan foothill landscape where the Shivalik ranges, Bhabar belt and adjoining Terai plains create a mosaic of habitats.

Mammals & Apex Predators

Home to the Royal Bengal Tiger, Asian Elephants (herds of 30+ individuals), Indian Leopards, Sloth Bears, Himalayan Black Bears, Jungle Cats, Fishing Cats, Barking Deer (Kakar), Spotted Deer (Chital), Sambars, and Hog Deer.

Avian Richness (500+ Bird Species)

Renowned globally among ornithologists. Spot resident and migratory species including the Great Pied Hornbill, Pallas’s Fish Eagle, Crested Serpent Eagle, Tawny Fish Owl, Red Junglefowl, Kalij Pheasant, and Kingfishers.

Aquatic & River Reptiles

The Ramganga river system and reservoir provide aquatic habitat for fish, gharials in suitable stretches, mugger crocodiles, turtles and other river-associated wildlife. Species presence varies by location and season.

Seasonal Travel Planner

Best Time to Visit Jim Corbett National Park

Each season paints Corbett in distinct hues — from golden winter mists to thrilling summer waterhole vigils.

❄️ Winter Season (Nov to Feb)

  • Temperature: 5°C to 25°C (Chilly mornings, sunny afternoons).
  • Highlights: All core zones (including Dhikala) are open. Best season for family trips, birdwatching, and river picnics.
  • Dress Code: Heavy woollens, windcheaters, gloves, and caps for open Gypsy morning drives.

☀️ Summer Season (Mar to Jun)

  • Temperature: 22°C to 40°C.
  • Highlights: Forest dry foliage opens visibility. Predators and elephant herds congregate near Ramganga and waterholes.
  • Wildlife Activity: Frequent wildlife movement near waterholes; sightings remain natural and never guaranteed. Carry hats, sunglasses, and light cottons.

🌧️ Monsoon Season (Jul to Oct)

  • Landscape: Emerald green vegetation, swelling mountain rivers.
  • Zone Status: Core zones (Dhikala & Bijrani) close for wildlife breeding. Jhirna, Dhela, and Phato remain open (subject to rain notices).
  • Vibe: Serene, uncrowded, and budget-friendly for quiet resort getaways.
Forest Ethics

Travel with Respect: Corbett Forest Rules & Do's and Don'ts

You are entering a protected sanctuary where conservation always takes precedence over tourism.

✅ Mandatory Do's

  • Listen to Your Guide: Follow the instructions of your registered nature guide and forest driver at all times.
  • Carry Back All Trash: Corbett is a zero-plastic zone. Take back all plastic bottles and snack wrappers.
  • Maintain Silence: Speak in whispers. Loud noises disturb wildlife and frighten animals away.

❌ Strict Don'ts

  • Never Alight from Vehicle: Getting down from your Gypsy inside the park is illegal, dangerous, and punishable by heavy fines.
  • No Feeding Wildlife: Feeding disrupts natural hunting and grazing habits.
  • No Flash Photography: Camera flashes startle big cats and aggressive elephant herds.

📋 Essential Information

  • Sightings are Natural: Sightings are 100% unpredictable. Enjoy the forest atmosphere, tracks, and birdlife.
  • Non-Govt Operator: Adventure Corbett Safari is an independent private tour operator. Official permits are managed at corbettgov.org.
  • Guide Fee Rule: Forest guide fee (₹700–₹1,000) is paid directly by clients in cash at the gate.
